Make AI useful for teaching

Step 1 · Better instructions

The CLASS prompting framework

Give the AI what you already know about your pupils, subject and lesson. A better brief makes the learning goal, activity and expected quality explicit.

C
Context

Set the scene.

  • Subject, year and topic
  • Prior knowledge
  • Relevant constraints
L
Learning

Describe the purpose.

  • Objective
  • What pupils should know or do
A
Activity

Specify what you need.

  • Type of task
  • Time and format
  • Style or resources
S
Support

Plan for the class.

  • Scaffolding
  • SEND or EAL needs
  • Challenge and misconceptions
S
Success

Define quality.

  • Expected output
  • Assessment evidence
  • Retrieval or exit ticket

See the difference

Too vague

“Create a lesson on photosynthesis.”

The result may be generic because the AI doesn't know your objective, prior learning or class needs.

Useful CLASS brief

Year 8 Science. Explain photosynthesis using the word equation. Use explicit teaching, guided practice and an independent task. Address the misconception that plants get their food from soil, support weaker readers and finish with a five-question exit ticket.

The workshop also illustrates CLASS with a Year 8 Computer Science lesson on converting 8-bit binary into denary.

Step 2 · Make something teachable

Three practical demonstrations

1. Better brief, better output

Start with a vague request. Then add the year group, objective, prior knowledge, lesson format, support and exit assessment.

Workshop example: Year 8 photosynthesis, including the 'food from soil' misconception.

2. Improve, don't regenerate

Keep the original objective but ask AI for three specific ways to strengthen an activity. Choose one, then refine it.

Workshop example: evaluating the most significant cause of the First World War.

3. Make something for tomorrow

Start with misconceptions, turn them into diagnostic hinge questions, then create a short aligned exit ticket.

Workshop example: adding and subtracting negative numbers in Year 7 Maths.

A useful follow-up

“Keep the best idea, but make sure pupils still have to think rather than simply copy information.”

This captures the workshop's emphasis on improving learning, not simply producing more polished worksheets.

Step 3 · Ready-to-use prompts

Prompt bank

Adapt the wording to your subject, year group and learning objective. Each example is designed to be refined rather than accepted uncritically.

Lesson planning

“Create a 50-minute lesson for Year [ ] on [topic] with explicit teaching, guided practice, independent practice, differentiation and an exit ticket.”

Improve an activity

“Suggest three ways to improve this activity without changing the learning objective: [paste activity].”

Misconceptions

“List likely misconceptions about [topic] and give a diagnostic question for each.”

Hinge questions

“Create five multiple-choice hinge questions on [topic] with plausible distractors.”

Scaffolding

“Scaffold this task for lower-attaining pupils without reducing the thinking.”

Exit tickets

“Create a five-minute exit ticket aligned to this objective: [objective].”

Quick fixes when the first answer misses the mark

Too generic?

“Make this more specific to my context: [year, topic, students, etc.].”

Too long?

“Keep this, but make it more concise.”

Not enough detail?

“Add timings, differentiation and full resources.”

Wrong format?

“Convert this into a PowerPoint, worksheet or knowledge organiser.”

Not quite right?

“Improve this, focusing on [specific element].”

Try again.

“Keep the good ideas, but improve the accuracy and relevance to my context.”

Step 4 · Keep the teacher in charge

Three essential safeguards

  1. Professional judgement: AI can suggest activities and explanations. You decide what pupils should learn, how they should learn it and which materials are good enough.
  2. Pupil privacy: do not paste identifiable or confidential information into public AI tools. Follow current school and trust guidance on approved services.
  3. Check accuracy: review explanations, answer keys, misconceptions, sources and age suitability before using outputs with pupils.
Workshop challenge: choose a lesson you genuinely plan to teach. Use AI to create or improve at least three useful elements, such as retrieval, an explanation, a model, a scaffold or an exit ticket. Review and refine what it produces.

The original workshop included a list of tools approved at the time. Always check the trust's current guidance, as approval and data protection arrangements can change.

Take it into your next lesson

Start with a clear brief, evaluate the first draft and improve the parts that need work. The aim is not to generate more lessons, but to reduce production time and focus more attention on learning.
